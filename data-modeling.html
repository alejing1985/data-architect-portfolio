01
The four levels
of data modeling
From most abstract to most concrete — semantic sits orthogonal to the other three
Why this matters in practice
"Most data quality problems I've seen aren't technical — they're modeling problems that were never surfaced at the right level. A disagreement between Marketing and Finance on what 'active customer' means is a semantic modeling problem, not a pipeline bug."
Conceptual
What exists
Language of the business · no technology
Question it answers
What entities exist and how do they relate?
Example
A Customer places Orders. An Order contains Products.
Artefact
Entity-relationship sketch · business glossary
Who needs it
Business stakeholders, domain owners, architects
Logical
How it's structured
Technology-agnostic · attributes and rules
Question it answers
What attributes does each entity have and what are the constraints?
Example
Customer (id, name, email, status). Order (id, date, customer_id FK, total).
Artefact
Normalized ERD · data dictionary
Who needs it
Data architects, data engineers, analysts
Physical
How it's stored
Engine-specific · performance and cost
Question it answers
How do we store this in the actual engine — partitioning, clustering, file format?
Example
Snowflake table clustered by order_date · partitioned Iceberg table on event_month.
Artefact
DDL scripts · dbt models · IaC definitions
Who needs it
Data engineers, platform architects
Semantic
What it means
Sits orthogonal · shared meaning across consumers
Question it answers
What does "active customer" mean — and does it mean the same thing in Marketing and Finance?
Example
Business glossary in Atlan · metric definitions in dbt Semantic Layer · ontologies.
Artefact
Glossary · taxonomy · dbt semantic layer · Atlan business terms
Who needs it
All consumers — it's governance infrastructure
Why semantic sits orthogonal
Unlike the other three levels which form a progression from abstract to concrete, semantic modeling cuts across all of them. You can have a perfect physical model and still have different teams reporting different revenue numbers because no one agreed on what "closed deal" means. Semantic modeling is the layer that prevents that — and it's the one most teams skip until the pain is already visible in dashboards.
02
Modeling patterns —
when to use which
Star Schema · Data Vault · One Big Table
My honest take
In my projects I've worked primarily with Star Schema because the use cases were reporting-driven and the organizational maturity didn't require anything more complex. That's not a limitation — it's the right call for most analytics platforms that aren't at hyperscale or in heavily regulated industries. Choosing a pattern more complex than what you need is just another form of over-engineering.
Star Schema
Kimball · dimensional modeling
Best for
Analytical reporting, BI tools, use cases where query performance and simplicity matter most. The structure is intuitive — business users can understand it.
Core idea
A central fact table (measurable events) surrounded by dimension tables (context — who, what, when, where). Denormalized for read performance.
Trade-off
Less flexible for late-arriving changes. If a business rule changes retroactively, you may need to reprocess history. Not designed for full auditability of every change.
In my experience
Used it in both platform migrations. Worked well for Gold layer data products oriented toward BI consumption in Power BI.
Used in production
Data Vault 2.0
Hub · Satellite · Link
Best for
Environments with strict auditability requirements — banking, insurance, regulated industries — where you need to trace every change to every record over time.
Core idea
Hubs (business keys), Links (relationships between hubs), Satellites (descriptive attributes with full history). Everything is insert-only — nothing is ever overwritten.
Trade-off
High complexity. More tables, more joins, steeper learning curve for the team. Query performance requires careful design of the information marts on top.
When I'd choose it
If the platform serves a regulated industry where full lineage and auditability of every data change is a non-negotiable requirement, not just a nice-to-have.
Architectural knowledge
One Big Table
OBT · wide table · denormalized
Best for
High-volume analytical workloads where join cost is the bottleneck and query simplicity for end users matters — common in modern cloud data warehouses at scale.
Core idea
Pre-join everything into a single wide table. Pay the cost once at ingestion, not at every query. Columnar engines like Snowflake or BigQuery handle wide tables efficiently.
Trade-off
Data duplication. Harder to maintain when source schemas change. Can become unwieldy as the number of dimensions grows. Not suitable for normalized update patterns.
When I'd choose it
For specific Gold layer data products where the consumer is a data scientist or ML pipeline that needs everything pre-joined and query simplicity beats storage efficiency.
Architectural knowledge
How to choose — a simple decision frame
Use Star Schema when
The primary use case is BI reporting · the team is not data engineering-heavy · query performance for business users is the priority · organizational maturity is moderate
Use Data Vault when
You're in a regulated industry (banking, insurance, pharma) · full auditability of every change is a compliance requirement · the data team is mature enough to operate it
Use OBT when
You're building for ML/data science consumers · the volume justifies pre-joining · you're using a columnar engine that handles wide tables well · join cost is measurably the bottleneck
03
Schema drift
vs schema evolution
Two different problems that are often confused — and require different solutions
Schema drift
The problem · uncontrolled change
Schema drift is what happens when source systems change their data structure without telling anyone — new columns appear, fields get renamed, data types change. Your pipelines break silently or load incorrect data.

This is the real-world scenario I've faced: sources changing formats without notification, causing downstream failures that are only discovered when a dashboard goes wrong.
Schema evolution
The capability · controlled adaptation
Schema evolution is the deliberate, controlled management of schema changes over time — absorbing changes from sources without breaking downstream consumers, and doing so in a way that is tracked, auditable and reversible.

The goal is not to prevent change — sources will always change. The goal is to make your platform resilient to change by design.
The real challenge
"The technical problem of schema evolution is largely solved by modern tooling. The governance problem — knowing that a change happened, understanding its impact, and deciding whether to absorb it or reject it — is not solved by any tool out of the box. That's what you need to build."
What the tooling handles — and what it doesn't
Delta Lake (Azure · Platform 01)
mergeSchema — used via ADF to automatically merge incoming schema changes into existing Delta tables. New columns are added, existing ones preserved.
Schema on write — Delta enforces the schema at write time, which catches unexpected changes before they corrupt data.
Time travel — every version of the table is preserved, so schema changes can be rolled back by reading a previous snapshot.
ACID transactions — schema changes are atomic, so partial writes on schema mismatch never leave the table in an inconsistent state.
overwriteSchema requires explicit opt-in — prevents accidental destructive changes but adds friction for intentional ones.
Apache Iceberg (Platform 02)
Add columns — metadata-only, no data rewrite. Old files return NULL for new columns.
Drop columns — removed from schema, existing data files untouched.
Rename columns — safe because Iceberg tracks columns by unique ID, not by name or position.
Type widening — e.g. int → bigint. No data rewrite required.
Breaking changes (narrowing types, dropping required columns) still need manual management.
Snowflake (Platform 02)
INFER_SCHEMA — automatically detects schema from staged files (Parquet, JSON, CSV, Avro).
Schema evolution via COPY INTO — detects new columns and adds them to the target table automatically.
Snowpipe Streaming — supports schema detection and evolution for streaming ingestion with Kafka connector.
Limited to COPY INTO and Snowpipe — INSERT operations cannot evolve the target table schema automatically.
We evaluated COPY INTO but it didn't fit our ingestion pattern, which relied on INSERT operations.
Our approach — governance layer on top of the tooling
Because neither Snowflake's COPY INTO nor Iceberg's native evolution covered our ingestion pattern fully, we built a governance layer as a Stored Procedure that sits between source ingestion and the Bronze layer:
01 · Detect
Schema comparison on ingestion
On every load, the SP compares the incoming file schema against the registered schema for that source. Any difference is flagged — new columns, renamed fields, type changes.
02 · Decide
Controlled absorption or rejection
Not every change is absorbed automatically. Additive changes (new columns) are absorbed. Breaking changes (dropped columns, type narrowing) trigger an alert and halt ingestion until reviewed.
03 · Document
Change log and downstream impact
Every schema change is logged with timestamp, source, change type and affected downstream data products. This closes the governance loop — the change is not just handled, it's traceable.
The principle: tooling handles the mechanics of change; governance handles the meaning of change. Snowflake and Iceberg tell you how to absorb a schema change without breaking things. Your governance layer tells you whether you should, and what it affects downstream.
Technology stack for schema management
Apache Iceberg Snowflake Delta Lake Parquet Atlan (lineage tracking) Stored Procedures Azure DevOps (schema validation gates)
04
Idempotency in
data pipelines
Designing pipelines that can be safely re-run without producing duplicate or inconsistent data
What it means
An idempotent pipeline produces the same result regardless of how many times it runs with the same input. Whether it runs once, fails midway and retries, or is re-triggered manually — the output is identical and the data isn't duplicated.

This is not a nice-to-have. In a data platform where pipelines fail and get retried automatically, a non-idempotent pipeline will silently corrupt data every time it recovers from a failure.
Why Bronze is the right layer to enforce it
Idempotency must be enforced at the ingestion layer — Bronze — because that's where data first enters the platform and where duplicates are cheapest to prevent. If a duplicate survives Bronze, it propagates through Silver and Gold and corrupts every data product downstream.

Fixing duplicates in Gold is exponentially more expensive than preventing them at the source boundary. The further downstream you detect the problem, the more surface area you have to repair.
The practical reality
"Every pipeline will be re-run at some point — scheduled retries, manual reruns after a failure, backfills after a source fix. If your Bronze layer isn't idempotent, each of those retries is a data quality incident waiting to happen."
Ingestion flow — source to Bronze
Idempotency is enforced across two hops, not one. Understanding the full flow is key to understanding where and why each control exists:
01
Source
Origin system. No guarantees — sources can re-send, rename or silently change files without notification.
02
Landing (ADLS · Parquet)
Raw files written to ADLS as Parquet files. Immutable raw record of what the source sent. Read-only — nothing is transformed here.
03
Metadata table
Before processing any file, the pipeline checks this table. Controls what has already been ingested — the gatekeeper between Landing and Bronze.
04
Bronze
Platform 01: Delta files on ADLS.
Platform 02: Iceberg tables in Snowflake.
Idempotent writes via MERGE on business key.
The metadata table — ingestion control layer
The metadata table is the primary idempotency mechanism. Before the pipeline processes any Parquet file from the Landing zone, it queries this table to determine whether that file has already been ingested and with what outcome.
Fields tracked
filename — exact name of the Parquet file in the Landing zone
timestamp — when the file arrived in Landing
status — pending / processing / processed / failed
Checksum — known gap. Without a checksum, a source replacing a file with the same name but different content would be silently skipped as already processed. Adding file-level checksum validation is a planned improvement.
Pipeline logic per status
Not found or pending — process the file, write to Bronze via MERGE, update status to processed.
Processed — skip. Already ingested successfully. Safe to ignore on retry.
Failed — retry. File was picked up but something went wrong. Re-process from scratch — safe because MERGE is idempotent.
Processing — flag for review. Pipeline died mid-run. Needs manual intervention before retry.
The metadata table also serves as an ingestion audit log — every file that has passed through the platform is recorded with its full processing history. This closes the lineage loop from source file to Bronze record.
MERGE at Bronze — idempotent writes
Once the metadata table confirms a file should be processed, the write to Bronze uses a MERGE on the business key — not a plain INSERT. This ensures that even if the same file is processed twice, the result is identical:
MATCHED
Record already exists
Data changed → UPDATE. Data identical → do nothing. No duplicate is created either way.
NOT MATCHED
New record
No match on business key → INSERT. Genuinely new record, not a retry of an existing one.
NOT MATCHED BY SOURCE
Record disappeared
Depending on the data contract: soft-delete or leave untouched. Always an explicit decision, never a side effect of a retry.
Delta Lake MERGE (Platform 01)
Parquet files from the Landing zone were converted to Delta files on ADLS via MERGE INTO, orchestrated through ADF. Delta's ACID guarantees meant a failed MERGE left the table in a consistent state — no partial writes. Time travel acted as a safety net: unexpected results could be rolled back to the previous snapshot before reprocessing.
Snowflake MERGE (Platform 02)
Parquet files from the Landing zone are loaded into Iceberg tables in Snowflake via Snowflake's MERGE. The business key is defined per source in the data product template. dbt models in Silver use unique_key to enforce idempotency at transformation time as well.
Key requirements for an idempotent Bronze layer
A reliable business key
The MERGE needs a key to match on. If the source doesn't provide a stable natural key, you need a surrogate key strategy first. No key, no idempotency.
A defined data contract
You need to know what "the same record" means for each source. Is it the same if the value changed? Is a soft-deleted record the same as an active one? This lives in the data product template, not in the pipeline code.
ACID-capable storage
MERGE only works safely with ACID guarantees. Delta Lake and Snowflake both provide this. The Landing zone (plain Parquet) is intentionally read-only — Bronze is always the ACID layer.
