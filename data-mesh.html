01
What Data Mesh
actually is
And why treating it as a technology decision is the first mistake
The most common misconception
"Data Mesh is not a new architecture for your lake or warehouse. It's a redefinition of how the company operates around data — who owns it, who is responsible for its quality, and how teams collaborate across domain boundaries. If that organizational change doesn't happen, no amount of technology makes it work."
The four principles (Zhamak Dehghani)
Domain ownership — each business domain owns its data end-to-end, including quality and delivery.

Data as a product — data is treated with the same product thinking as software: it has an owner, an SLA, and consumers.

Self-serve data platform — a central platform provides the infrastructure so domains can operate autonomously without depending on a central data team.

Federated computational governance — global standards (naming, contracts, security) enforced across all domains without centralizing decision-making.
What it requires organizationally
Domain teams with data ownership — each domain needs people who understand both the business and the data. Without that, ownership is a label, not a reality.

Management alignment — Data Mesh changes how teams are funded, measured and held accountable. Without executive buy-in, the model collapses under organizational inertia.

A data platform team — someone still needs to build and maintain the shared infrastructure that makes domain autonomy possible. That team shifts from delivery to enablement.

Time — this is a multi-year transformation, not a migration project.
The maturity spectrum — where most organizations actually are
Full Data Mesh is the right destination for organizations that have reached a certain level of data maturity. Most haven't. The honest move is to design toward Data Mesh while operating at the level the organization can actually sustain.
Level 1
Centralized
Central data team owns and builds everything. All domains depend on it. Common starting point — unsustainable at scale.
Level 2 · Current
Centralized-Federated
Central team builds all data products but architecture is designed for domain ownership when maturity allows. Self-service enabled but not yet fully exploited.
● Where we are now
Level 3 · Target
Federated
Domains own their data products. Central team provides platform and governance standards. Shared infrastructure, distributed ownership.
◎ Where we're heading
Level 4
Full Data Mesh
Full domain autonomy, federated governance, self-serve platform. Requires high organizational maturity across every domain.
● Level 2
Level 3 ◎ — current trajectory
The key insight: the architecture should be designed for Level 4 even if you're operating between Level 2 and Level 3. Decoupled layers, domain-oriented data products, and self-serve access in Gold cost nothing extra to build — and they're the difference between a platform that enables growth and one that needs to be rebuilt when the organization matures.
When Data Mesh fails — the real reasons
01 · Most common
Treated as a technology migration, not an organizational transformation
The decision to adopt Data Mesh is made by the data team and implemented as an architecture change. Business domains are never meaningfully involved. The technology changes; the operating model doesn't. Within months, the central data team is still doing everything — just with a more complex stack.
02
No management alignment — internally or with stakeholders
Without alignment between data managers and clear communication with domain stakeholders, Data Mesh produces ownership on paper and confusion in practice. Domains don't know what's expected of them. The data team doesn't know what to build for whom. Governance becomes a bottleneck instead of an enabler.
03
Organizational maturity mismatch
Adopting full Data Mesh before the organization has the people, processes and culture to support domain ownership sets the project up to fail. The right response is to design for Data Mesh but operate at the maturity level you actually have — and make that decision explicit, not by default.
02
What a data product
actually is
Beyond the definition — what makes it work in practice
The shift that matters
"A data product is not a dataset with an owner assigned to it. It's a reusable, trustworthy unit of data that solves a problem for a defined set of consumers — and that other data products can build on. The shift from 'we deliver requirements' to 'we build products' is the hardest and most important change in a Data Mesh adoption."
Anatomy of a data product
👤
Owner
A named person or team accountable for the product. Not a committee. Without a single owner, no one is truly responsible for quality or SLA adherence.
📋
Data contract
Explicit agreement with consumers: schema, SLA (freshness, completeness, availability), and what changes require a versioning notice. The contract is what makes a dataset a product.
🔗
Defined consumers
A data product with no known consumers is infrastructure, not a product. Knowing who depends on it is what drives quality decisions and prioritization.
📐
Schema & semantics
Standardized naming conventions and documented meaning for every field. A column called "status" that means different things in different products is a governance failure.
🔍
Discoverability
Registered in the data catalog (Atlan, Purview). A data product that consumers can't find doesn't get reused — which defeats the entire purpose.
📦
Declared sources
Explicit documentation of upstream dependencies — other data products or raw sources. This is what enables lineage and impact analysis when something changes upstream.
Standardization is not optional
Before building the first data product, naming conventions and a shared definition of "what is a data product" must exist — understood by the data team, by management, and by domain stakeholders.

Without standardization, every team interprets data products differently. Some build one per business question. Others build one for the entire domain. Neither is wrong by itself — but without a standard, you can't reason about reuse, ownership, or governance at scale.

We formalized this with Excel templates before moving to YAML data contracts — a pragmatic step that allowed non-technical stakeholders to participate in definition before the tooling was in place.
Reuse is the goal — not coverage
The fundamental purpose of a data product is reusability. The question is not "does this data product exist?" but "is this data product being used as a building block by others?"

A Silver Master data product that feeds five Gold products is more valuable than five Silver products that each solve one specific requirement. The former is a platform asset. The latter is five pipelines with an owner label.

This shift — from solving requirements to building reusable assets — is the hardest cultural change in any Data Mesh adoption, and the one that takes the longest to internalize.

One of the most important concepts to establish early: a data product can be the source of another data product. This is what enables reuse at scale — and what most teams fail to internalize when they first adopt the pattern.

Datamart schema
The Datamart schema above Gold is a deliberate architectural decision — it separates analytical data products (Gold, internal platform consumption) from presentation data products (Datamart, Power BI reports and potential marketplace exposure). This boundary makes it explicit what is ready for end-user consumption and what is still platform infrastructure.
The granularity question — when is a Silver its own data product?
One of the hardest design decisions in practice: when does a Silver transformation deserve to be a standalone data product vs staying as an internal step inside another product? The answer depends on one question: is it reused?
Situation Decision Rationale
Silver feeds 2+ Gold data products Standalone Silver Master DP Reuse justifies the ownership and contract overhead. Changes to it have downstream impact that needs to be managed explicitly.
Silver feeds only one specific Gold Internal transformation, not a product No reuse means no consumer beyond the parent product. The overhead of a contract and owner is not justified.
Silver might be reused in future Design as a product, defer the contract Build with product discipline (naming, schema, documentation) but don't formalize the contract until there's a second consumer. Avoids over-engineering while keeping the option open.
Silver is complex but domain-specific Depends on organizational maturity In early stages, keep it internal. As the platform matures and cross-domain consumption increases, promote it to a standalone product with a formal contract.
There is no universal answer — the right granularity evolves with the platform and the organization. What matters is having an explicit decision framework so the choice is made consciously, not by default.
03
Self-service — what it
actually requires
Technology is the easy part. The hard part is organizational.
The common mistake
"Self-service doesn't happen because you expose a Gold layer. It happens when the organization has the knowledge, the processes, and the management support to use it — and when domain teams understand that building their own reports is their responsibility, not something they delegate to the data team."
Layer structure designed for self-service
Self-service requires a clear separation between what the data team owns and what domain teams consume. In our platform, that boundary sits between Gold and the Datamart schema:
Bronze
Raw ingestion. Parquet → Delta / Iceberg. Internal platform layer — never exposed directly.
Data team owns
Silver
Clean, validated data. Master and Specific variants. Internal platform layer — consumed by Gold data products.
Data team owns
Gold
Analytical data products. Domain-oriented, analytics-ready. Self-service enabled — domains can query directly when maturity allows.
Data team → domains
Datamart
Presentation layer above Gold. Models ready for Power BI consumption or marketplace exposure. The explicit boundary for end-user self-service.
Domains consume
The Datamart schema is not a technical necessity — it's a governance boundary. It makes explicit which models are production-ready for end-user consumption, separates platform concerns from presentation concerns, and creates a clean handoff point between the data team and domain consumers.
What an organization actually needs for self-service to work
🧠
Minimum data literacy in each domain
Each domain needs people who can read and work with data — at minimum a data analyst profile embedded in the team. Without this, self-service is not self-service — it's a support ticket to the data team with extra steps.
🏛️
Management support and clear ownership
Domain managers need to understand that building their own reports is their team's responsibility, not a service the data team provides. Without management alignment at domain level, self-service doesn't get prioritized.
📖
Documented, discoverable data products
If domain teams can't find what exists, understand what it contains, or trust its quality, they won't use it. Catalog, lineage and data quality are not governance overhead — they're prerequisites for self-service adoption.
🤝
Defined responsibilities between data team and domains
The boundary must be explicit: the data team delivers certified, well-documented data products in the Datamart schema. Domain analysts build domain-specific reports from those products. Organizational reports can be built centrally and used as a base. The handoff point is the contract.
The evolution path — from centralized to self-service
Self-service is not a switch you flip — it's a gradual transfer of responsibility as domain maturity increases:
Phase 1 · Now
Data team builds everything
Data team builds all data products and organizational-level reports. Domains consume but don't produce. Architecture is already designed for future ownership transfer.
Phase 2 · Next
Domains build on top
Domain analysts use certified Datamart products as a base to build domain-specific reports in Power BI. Data team focuses on platform and organizational products.
Phase 3 · Future
Domains own their products
Mature domains assume ownership of their Gold data products. Data team shifts fully to platform enablement, governance and cross-domain products.
